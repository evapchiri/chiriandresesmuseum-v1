February 2026

As I embarked on this project almost last-minute, and my time with my parents was limited to less than 2 weeks, I have to be strategic on what to spend time on — mostly as the nature of this project is 'learn-by-doing'. There are many things that, if I want to do it "professionally", I need to give some extra care at the beginning, to know how best to proceed.

First job: dust off the camera and revisit the basics of photography — ISO, aperture, all of it. It has been a little while. With a turntable, a set of LED lights, and my parents' table hule as a backdrop, I put together a surprisingly decent little studio.

Time wasn't on my side — I was days from flying back to Japan, with no idea when I'd see these objects again in the near future. So the priority became simple: photograph as many as possible. For some initial guidance on what to expect, I leaned on 3D4CH's webinar series Essential Guide to 3D Digitised Heritage — particularly the "Capturing and Processing 3D Data" session by Catherine Anne Cassidy of CARARE (they are a Europeana aggregator) for archaeological and architectural heritage — plus a YouTube walkthrough with some tips on photogrammetry processing that I had found along the way.

With the basics down, I still needed to test what actually worked with my setup. The first attempt was 23 photos of an object I nicknamed CHAN-002, which looked like a particularly difficult object. My thoughts are: if I can make this object work, then it should be easier on the ones that present less complexity. But…, this was a proper flop!

Photo alignment failed straight away — for the algorithm, every shot had been taken from roughly the same spot, so it had nothing to build a 3D cloud from.

I tried manually masking all 23 images in a separate piece of software (Affinity) to rescue it, but no luck.

My next guess was that there might not have been enough overlap between shots. So I shot a second object (CHAN-003) with smaller turning increments than with CHAN-002 — which aligned much better. Although the sides of the object still had some gaps, it's thus reasonable to think that fewer increments could fix that.

Tightening the increments even further (55 photos this time) got a much more detailed scan of the object's top — just not the rest, which I hadn't covered.

Finally, dropping close to the recommended 10-degree turning interval gave me my first real success — CHAN-001B, clean alignment, just over 42,000 tie points (the reference points the software uses to work out how all the photos relate to each other in 3D space).

That test-and-fail cycle took about two days, shooting, manually masking, and processing combined. I decided to then 'jump straight to the business' and start bulk-photographing. For the time I had left, I stuck to ~10-degree turns, and prioritised overlap and full angle coverage. Across what I called my "intense photography" period, I shot roughly 400 photos per object — front, top, bottom as a general rule — across 18 objects. Some were picked to test something different: one tiny, one with an open/closed state, one shot handheld, one pure reflective metal.

Looking back now, after the 2 weeks at my parents' house, and not having started on the processing stage yet, I do feel that slower would have been faster here. I'd have liked more time to actually understand my camera before diving in — I can't say I fully understood aperture settings, if I'm completely honest — and I spent that week fighting depth of field and focus while shooting any close-up shots. I got to a workable result quickly, which I'm glad about, but it was very much a "that'll do" rather than the true understanding that I'd preferred.
