Stage-by-stage

A closer walkthrough of what happened at each stage of the digitisation pipeline:

Stage 01

Project planning

The project's aim is quite straight-forward:

"To generate high-quality 3D models of a family heirloom and travel-souvenir collection to museum-standards for model, metadata and paradata, and make them accessible through a purpose-built web experience."

The project was originally divided as two initial strands:

  • EP-D001 — Digitisation campaigns: generating the 3D models
  • EP-W001 — Webapp: developing the digital platform to showcase the generated 3D models

These strands were designed to be both reiterated if needed (re-capturing objects that didn't work the first time) and expanded (e.g. EP-D002, etc...) as I do more digitisation rounds in the future, or I roll out new versions of the website.

As of the end of EP-001, the successfully digitised bunch (9 objects) is roughly only 1/6 of my parents' 60+ full collection, and I aim to expand their digital footprint in the future.

Because of this ever-expanding aim, there were a handful of objects that were also photographed purely as experiments, with no expectation of generating usable models to be exposed this first instance.

The project's documentation framework (i.e. deciding what and how things should be documented) was designed, adapted for a solo practitioner, following:

  • Best-practice standards set by the European Commission for best-quality digitisation processes in Cultural Heritage (set in the VIGIE 2020/654 study) which heavily focus on high-traceability.
  • The professional guidance provided by the 3D4CH Competence Centre's guidance — in particular its Essential Guide to 3D Digitised Heritage training series and the “Capturing and Processing 3D Data” session presented by Catherine Anne Cassidy of CARARE.

Every object was assessed for their complexity — its shape, its surface, its material — and that assessment decided both its capture approach and its minimum 'what good looks like' targets. The available non-professional equipment, needing to increase my photography and 3D modelling knowledge, and the narrow access window to the objects (< 2 weeks) influenced against these quality targets and completion deadlines.

Stage 02

Photography

Eighteen objects, photographed over a single week of access at my parents' home in Spain (16–22 February 2026), immediately before I moved to Japan. That deadline shaped everything that followed: the objects aren't reachable for a re-shoot, so whatever the photographs captured — or missed — is what every later stage had to work with. Several decisions further down the pipeline trace straight back to it.

Capture was turntable photogrammetry on a home rig against a white background, with the same kit across the whole set:

  • Camera: Canon 2000D DSLR, EF-S 18–55mm IS II lens
  • Lighting: 2× NEEWER dimmable LED panels, 5600K, with a white diffuser
  • Scale reference: homemade, accurate to ±1mm
  • Colour profile: sRGB IEC61966-2.1

Each object was shot in front, top and bottom passes, turning the turntable by hand in roughly 10–15° steps — around 15–20 minutes of shooting per object once the manual turning is counted in. Some objects doubled as deliberate tests: very shiny or reflective surfaces, handheld sequences, boxes photographed both open and closed.

I went in with a working but non-specialist grasp of photogrammetry, and a few techniques I now consider essential simply weren't on my radar yet. No cross-polarising filter was used — I didn't know the technique at the time — and no dedicated specularity capture was done. Both are planned for future campaigns, and both are the reason some of this set's material properties had to be reconstructed by hand later rather than measured from the capture itself.

Stage 03

Photogrammetry processing

This is where the week's photographs became models, using Agisoft Metashape Standard: aligning images into tie points, building the dense point cloud, then the mesh and the texture.

I came in knowing the broad shape of the process but not its settings, and much of this stage was learning — in real depth — what each parameter does and which ones matter for which kind of object. The workflow kept changing as challenges came up and got solved. The single biggest shift was moving from hand-made masks to an AI-assisted automatic masking step; together with a tidied-up order of operations, it brought a typical model down from about an hour and a half to roughly forty minutes, compute time included.

Not everything cooperated. The homemade scale markers, useful for accuracy, were themselves a recurring nuisance in alignment and masking. And three objects — CHAN-002, CHAN-004 and CHAN-005 — never reached a usable state; they're documented plainly as “what didn't survive, and why” rather than folded away.

This stage is also where HeritageScan came in — a small command-line tool Robert built on Apple's RealityKit, run against the same source images as a second opinion when Metashape struggled. It rescued none of the genuinely failed captures. Its one clear win was CHAN-013, which it reconstructed faster and more cleanly than the standard pipeline had managed. The lesson was blunt: a different algorithm can't make up for data the camera never recorded. Get the capture right — or at least shoot RAW.

A model was only counted among the finished nine if it hit the accuracy target and still read as a credible representation of the object without disproportionate manual repair. A model that merely existed wasn't enough. Nine cleared that bar.

Stage 04

Post-processing

Every finished model then passed through Blender for cleanup and finishing — retopology, UV unwrapping, normal and ambient-occlusion bakes, mesh repair — with InstantMeshes handling the retopology step. I'd never opened Blender before this project, so this stage was learnt from scratch.

The guiding rule was restraint. Geometry and texture fixes were kept to the minimum needed to make a model a fair representation of its object; if a model needed more than that, it wasn't treated as successful. The first versions I put on Sketchfab were the raw, un-retopologised meshes — heavy and inefficient — and were later replaced with clean-topology versions carrying coherent UV maps.

Roughness and metallic maps are authored at this stage, and this is where the project's central open question sits. Where the capture gave me something to work from, those values are derived from it; where it didn't, they're judged by eye against the physical object and flagged plainly as estimated. Two objects needed that hand-authoring — CHAN-007's gilding and CHAN-016's polished stones. The honest fix lies upstream: cross-polarised capture for a cleaner albedo, and empirical specularity methods such as Kintsugi 3D for roughness and metalness. That gap is what the next phase of the project is meant to close.

Stage 05

Metadata & paradata

With the models done, the records had to be pulled together. The principle here is one merged source of truth per object: the family-held metadata (materials, dates, measurements, provenance) and the paradata from digitisation (equipment, angles, image counts, Metashape's tie-point and reprojection figures, and the final deliverables) kept side by side rather than in separate silos.

Each finished object keeps its Metashape processing report as part of that record. Every value is marked for how it was arrived at — measured from the capture, estimated by eye, n/a, or flagged as a known limitation — so the confidence behind each number is visible rather than assumed.

This stage also carried the non-technical work: translating the family's accounts from Spanish into English, writing the story behind each object, and sorting the collection into categories and shared labels. The result is a single structured record per object, and a project archive holding the planning tables, journals and reports that the public pages are drawn from.

Stage 06

'Chiriandreses Museum' website

A 3D model on its own doesn't carry much. In cultural heritage especially, a scan without its context — where the object came from, what it meant, how it was captured and what that capture can and can't tell you — is a shape without a story. This site exists to put that context back around each model.

Version 1 is deliberately plain: hand-written HTML, CSS and JavaScript, no framework and no build step beyond a few small scripts. Two earlier attempts at this same site were built on heavier stacks and abandoned once the tooling outweighed the need. The scope here is the nine finished objects, each with its story, its “how it was made” panel and its full provenance record, linking out to the complete methodology. Later versions can add interactivity and other ways to move through the collection — better planned for once the collection has actually grown.

The build was co-created with a large language model, used as a working collaborator throughout — which feels fitting for a project whose second thread is about trust in digitally-mediated heritage.
